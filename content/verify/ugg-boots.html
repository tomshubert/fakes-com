Fake UGG boots are often sold through convincing copycat websites. UGG publishes its own guidance on spotting them. Here's what it says, in plain English.

Buy from the right places

UGG says the safest place to buy is UGG.com, and it also recommends UGG stores and reputable authorized retailers. Its counterfeit guide links to a store locator for finding authorized retailers. UGG says it won't accept exchanges, refunds or warranty claims for products bought from unauthorized sellers.

Signs of a fake UGG website

UGG lists these warning signs:

  • The web address. Odd letters or numbers in it, or a site that isn't secure (look for https). UGG says to assume products are fake on a site with "UGG" or a style name in its address, unless it's UGG's own site or an authorized retailer.
  • Sloppy writing. Spelling and grammar mistakes, or pages copied from UGG.com.
  • Hard to reach. Only a contact form, an unusual email address, generic chat replies, or a phone number nobody answers.
  • Missing policies. Terms, warranty or privacy pages that are missing or don't make sense.
  • Deep discounts that seem too good to be true.
  • Shipping straight from China. UGG says it doesn't ship products directly from China; everything goes through its own warehouse first.

Check the boots themselves

UGG suggests comparing what you received with the same style on UGG.com, and watching for:

  • Poorly stitched seams
  • Fur that sheds
  • Colors that don't match from one boot to the other
  • Packaging that doesn't look like what UGG.com shows

Pay so you can get your money back

UGG advises paying by credit or debit card, because your bank may let you dispute the charge. UGG itself can't help with disputes over counterfeit purchases.
