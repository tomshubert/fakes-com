A counterfeit handbag is a waste of money. A counterfeit phone charger, bike helmet or face cream can hurt someone. U.S. Customs and Border Protection has warned that fake products can overheat, break on impact, cause skin reactions or start fires.

Counterfeits can turn up anywhere online, including on well-known marketplaces. Here's how to tell what you're actually buying.

Warning signs before you buy

  • The price is too good. Name brands rarely sell far below their normal price. CBP lists steep discounts across a whole site as a warning sign.
  • The seller isn't the brand. On marketplaces, check who is actually selling and shipping the item, not just whose logo is in the photo.
  • It ships from overseas when the brand doesn't. CBP flags products shipping from outside the U.S. as a sign to look closer.
  • You found it through a social media ad. CBP advises being cautious about buying through social media and avoiding suspicious ads.
  • The photos look borrowed. If every photo looks copied from the brand's own website and none show the actual item, ask the seller for real photos.

Products where fakes are dangerous

CategoryWhy it mattersSafest place to buy
Chargers, batteries, cablesFakes can overheat or catch fireThe device maker or a major retailer selling directly
Cosmetics and skin careUnknown ingredients can cause reactionsThe brand's site or its authorized stores
Helmets and safety gearMay fail in a crashAuthorized dealers listed on the brand's website
Medicines and supplementsWrong dose, wrong ingredients, or none at allA licensed pharmacy

Tom's advice

Most brands publish a list of authorized sellers on their own website. Thirty seconds on that page tells you whether the shop you're looking at is legitimate. For anything that plugs in, goes on your skin, or protects your head, I don't take chances on an unknown seller to save a few dollars.

What about "dupes"?

A dupe is a different product that looks similar to a popular one and is sold under its own name. That's generally legal, though some look-alikes still cross the line. A counterfeit pretends to be the brand, with its logo and name. If a listing uses the real brand's name and logo at a dupe price, it's a counterfeit.

If you received a fake

  1. Stop using it, especially electronics or anything you'd put on your skin.
  2. Ask the marketplace for a refund and report the listing as counterfeit.
  3. If you paid by credit card, dispute the charge.
  4. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
