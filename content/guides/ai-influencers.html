She posts every day. Perfect skin, perfect kitchen, a warm voice in her videos. She's a "nutritionist," and she swears by a supplement you can buy through the link in her bio. Thousands of people comment. She even answers your message.

She may not exist.

AI can now create a realistic person from scratch: photos, videos, even replies to your messages. Some of these personas are openly labeled entertainment or marketing. Others are built to pass as real people so they can sell you something, take your money or win your trust.

The numbers behind it

The FTC says people reported losing $2.1 billion in 2025 to scams that started on social media, eight times more than in 2020. Instagram ranked third for losses, after Facebook and WhatsApp.

What fake personas are used for

The FBI has warned that criminals use AI to make realistic photos for fake social media profiles, and that they use images of celebrities and online personalities to promote counterfeit goods and products that never arrive. Security researchers describe the same personas being used to:

  • Sell health products. An AI "doctor" or "expert" promotes supplements, backed by comments and messages that feel personal but are automated.
  • Push investments. A confident persona shares crypto tips or "analysis." The FTC says investment scams were the biggest money-loser on social media in 2025, at $1.1 billion.
  • Sell bargains. Shopping scams were the most-reported kind of social media scam. Many started with an ad.
  • Start a relationship. The FBI says scammers send AI-made private photos and videos so you believe you're talking to a real person.

Warning signs

  • Too polished, all the time. Every photo is flawless and on-brand, and nothing is ever messy or ordinary.
  • Small glitches. The FBI says to look for distorted hands or feet, odd teeth or eyes, wrong shadows, and unnatural movement in video.
  • Credentials you can't check. "Doctor" or "financial expert," with no name you can look up in a license or professional registry.
  • Everything leads to a purchase. Every post, comment and reply steers you toward a link, a product or a coin.
  • They come to you. A friendly direct message from an account you never contacted, followed by an offer.
  • Payment you can't undo. Requests for cryptocurrency, gift cards, wire transfers or payment apps.

A real-looking face proves nothing. AI images keep getting better, and the absence of glitches doesn't mean a person is real.

Look for the label

Instagram now has an "AI-generated profile" label for accounts that feature an AI-generated person. In August 2026, Instagram said it will limit how often unlabeled accounts like these are recommended. The label appears on the profile and next to its posts. If you see it, you're looking at a character, not a person. If you don't see it, that doesn't prove the person is real: not every AI account gets labeled.

The FTC also bans fake reviews and testimonials, including AI-generated ones, and bans buying or selling fake followers or views to look more influential for business. A huge following is not proof of anything.

Three habits that protect you

  1. Don't buy through the link in the post. Look the company up yourself. The FTC suggests searching its name with "scam" or "complaint" first.
  2. Never let someone you met on social media steer your money. That's the FTC's advice for investments, and it holds for any "opportunity."
  3. Never send money to someone you know only online. The FBI's advice covers cash, gift cards, crypto and anything else of value.

Not sure about a post, profile or offer? Check it here before you click, pay or believe it.

If you already paid

  1. Contact your bank or card company right away and ask about reversing the charge.
  2. Report the account to Instagram, or wherever you found it.
  3. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to the FBI at IC3.gov.
