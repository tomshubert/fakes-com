The phone rings. It's your grandson, and he sounds terrified. He's been in an accident, or arrested, and he needs money right now. Please don't tell Mom and Dad.

It sounds exactly like him. It might not be him at all.

This is the family-emergency scam, one of the oldest tricks in fraud. What's new is that scammers can now use AI to copy a person's voice, sometimes from just a short clip posted online. The FTC has warned that this can make the call sound just like your loved one.

The numbers behind it

In 2025, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center counted more than 22,000 complaints involving AI, with reported losses of about $893 million.

How the scam works

The FTC describes the same pattern again and again:

  • Urgency. You're told something terrible has happened and you are the only one who can help, right now.
  • Secrecy. You're asked not to tell anyone, so you won't call another relative who could expose the lie.
  • A second "official." A supposed lawyer, police officer or doctor gets on the line to make it feel real.
  • Real details. Scammers often know names and family details from social media or a hacked email account.
  • Hard-to-reverse payment. They want a wire transfer, cryptocurrency, a payment app, or gift card numbers read over the phone.

Three steps that stop it

  1. Hang up and call back. Use a phone number you already have for the person, not the one that called you. If they answer and they're fine, you're done.
  2. Check with someone else. Call another family member to confirm the story, even if the caller begged you to keep it secret. Secrecy is the scammer's protection, not your relative's.
  3. Ask something only they would know. If you can't hang up, ask a question a stranger couldn't answer from social media.

Tom's advice: pick a family code word today

Choose a word or phrase that everyone in your family knows and nobody posts online. If someone calls in a panic asking for money, ask for the code word. A cloned voice can say anything except a secret it doesn't know. It takes two minutes to set up, and it's the single best protection I know of.

How to make your voice harder to copy

  • Make social media accounts private, especially ones with videos of you or your children talking.
  • Be careful with voicemail greetings and public videos that contain long, clear recordings of your voice.
  • Talk with older relatives about this scam before it happens, so a panicked call doesn't catch them off guard.

If you already sent money

  1. Contact the company you used to send it (your bank, the wire service, the gift card company or the payment app) immediately and ask them to stop or reverse it. Speed matters.
  2. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to the FBI at IC3.gov.
  3. Report it to your state attorney general.
