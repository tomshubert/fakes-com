Your phone rings. The caller says they're with the U.S. Marshals or your local police, and that a warrant is out for your arrest because you skipped jury duty. You can avoid jail, they say, if you pay a fine right away.

It's a scam. Courts and police don't work this way, but the call is designed to scare you into paying before you have time to think. Our Scam Alerts page has tracked recent warnings from sheriff's offices and police about callers like this.

The numbers behind it

The FTC says some fake "court" websites used in this scam have asked people to pay as much as $10,000 in fines.

How the scam works

  • An urgent call out of the blue. According to the FTC, most of these scams start with a call from someone posing as a U.S. Marshal or a local officer. Caller ID may even show your police department's name, because caller ID can be faked.
  • Details that make it sound real. The caller may already know your full name or home address.
  • A threat and a deadline. You're told you missed jury duty (you almost certainly didn't) and will be arrested unless you pay now. Hanging up, they may say, will make it worse.
  • A fake warrant. In a newer version, the FTC says scammers text or email a document made to look like an official arrest warrant, listing what you supposedly owe.
  • A fake payment website. Some callers send you to a site with an official-sounding address and government-style seals. It asks for your birthdate and Social Security number to "look up" your fine, then asks you to pay online or at a so-called government kiosk using cryptocurrency.

The goal is your money, your personal details, or both. A Social Security number and birthdate are enough to open the door to identity theft.

Warning signs

  • Someone calls to say you'll be arrested. The FTC says real officers won't call to warn you of an arrest or threaten you if you hang up.
  • You're asked to pay over the phone. Courts don't collect payments by phone, and neither does any government agency.
  • The payment method is unusual. Only scammers insist you pay with gift cards, cash, a payment app, cryptocurrency or a wire transfer service like Western Union or MoneyGram.
  • You get a "warrant" by text or email. Real law enforcement never sends arrest warrants that way.
  • You're asked for your Social Security number or birthdate. The federal courts say they don't require sensitive information by phone or email, and that most of their contact with possible jurors happens through regular mail.
  • You never got a summons. If this is the first you've heard of jury duty, that's a strong clue.

What to do

  1. Hang up. Don't pay, and don't give out any personal information.
  2. Don't visit any website the caller gives you. Don't call back a number they give you either.
  3. Check it yourself. If you're worried you really did miss jury service, look up your court's official jury information page on your own, or call the court's clerk at a number you know is real. For federal courts, the U.S. Courts website has a court finder that lists each clerk's office.
  4. Report it. Tell the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If the caller claimed to be from a federal court, the U.S. Courts also ask you to let the clerk of the local U.S. District Court know. Pretending to be a federal court official is a crime.

If you already paid or shared information

The FTC says it's always worth asking the company you used to send money whether it can get it back.

  1. Card: call your card issuer or bank using the number on the back of the card and ask for a refund.
  2. Gift card: call the company that issued it, explain a scammer tricked you, and ask for a refund. Keep the card and receipt.
  3. Payment app or wire transfer: report it to the app or the wire company right away and ask them to reverse the payment.
  4. Cryptocurrency: contact the exchange or crypto ATM operator, tell them the transaction was fraud, and ask them to reverse it. Recovery can be hard because crypto lacks the protections cards have.
  5. Social Security number: if you gave it out, go to IdentityTheft.gov for steps to protect yourself or a recovery plan if it has been misused.
  6. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
