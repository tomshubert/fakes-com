A shocking photo of a plane crash. A moving video of a veteran at a parade. A post saying officials just "busted" a fraud scheme. Each spread fast in early November 2026, and each turned out to be fake or misleading. Fact-checkers found that the photo had been "cleaned up" with AI, the video was made by AI, and the fraud clip was a press conference from two years earlier.

You don't need special skills to avoid being fooled. A few habits catch most of these.

Tom's advice

The most powerful check is the simplest one: pause before you share. Fakes spread because they make us feel something right away, whether outrage, pride or fear. If a post makes you want to share it instantly, that's exactly the moment to take one more minute.

Fake and AI-made photos

  • Look at the details. AI often slips on hands, ears, teeth and arms, with extra or missing parts. Google also suggests looking for garbled text, shadows that don't match, and patterns that repeat unnaturally.
  • Be wary of a picture that's too perfect. One viral photo of the November 1 jet crash in Florida looked sharper than any real footage. It appears to have been "enhanced" with AI, making it look as if a wing was missing. Real footage shows both wings.
  • Find where it first appeared. A reverse image search, such as Google's, shows where else a picture has been posted. Often you'll find the original, an older date, or a fact check.
  • Look for an invisible label. Some AI tools hide an invisible watermark in what they make. Google's Gemini app can tell you whether an image, video or audio file carries Google's watermark, called SynthID. The Content Credentials Verify tool shows a file's history if it has one, including whether AI was used.

No tool can prove a photo is real

Google says that if its check finds no watermark, the file wasn't made with Google's AI, but another AI tool may have made it. A file with no Content Credentials isn't proof of anything either. And the UK fact-checker Full Fact says that, so far, it hasn't found online "AI detectors" to be reliable. Use these tools as clues, not verdicts.

AI-made and misleading videos

  • Slow it down and watch the edges. In an AI video of a "Veterans Day parade," fact-checkers spotted drumsticks vanishing from a girl's hands, medals melting into a uniform and a flag whose stripes changed width as it waved.
  • Check the date, not just the content. Real video with a new caption is one of the most common tricks. A clip shared as proof of 2026 voter fraud in Pennsylvania was a 2024 press conference about the previous election.
  • Remember that "it's AI" can be a lie too. Real dashcam video of the Florida jet crash was falsely called "100% AI." Major news outlets had confirmed it, and it was traced to the person who filmed it.

Rumors and viral claims

  • Ask who's behind it. Is the post from a news outlet, an official agency, or an account you know nothing about?
  • Ask whether it makes sense. Would something this big be on the news? When fake photos showed world landmarks lit blue for an election, the landmarks' own websites and the news showed nothing.
  • See if it has already been checked. Search the claim along with the words "fact check." Established fact-checkers such as Lead Stories, PolitiFact, AP and Reuters often check viral claims quickly. Several of the examples on this page were checked within a day of being posted.
  • Go to the original source. If a post quotes an agency, a company or a public figure, check their own website or official account.

If you already shared it

It happens to everyone. Delete the post if you can, and add a short note saying it turned out to be false. That helps stop it spreading further among the people who trust you.
