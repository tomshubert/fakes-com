The "Email From the Boss" Scam: How to Stop a Fake Payment Request

Business email compromise tricks employees into wiring money, changing payment details or buying gift cards. One phone call stops it.

By Tom Shubert · Updated Nov 9, 2026

The email looks routine. Your boss needs a wire sent before the end of the day. A long-time vendor has "new bank details" for this month's invoice. The owner, who is "stuck in a meeting," asks you to pick up some gift cards for a client and promises to pay you back. Every one of these can be a scam, and the FBI calls it business email compromise, or BEC.

I've seen this happen to careful people at well-run businesses. The money went out because the request looked normal and came from someone they trusted. Nobody was careless. The scam is built to look like everyday work.

How big this is

In its 2025 report, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) received 24,768 business email compromise complaints, with reported losses of about $3 billion. Only investment fraud cost victims more.

How the scam works

Criminals pose as someone you already deal with: your boss, a coworker, a supplier, a title company or a lawyer. They do it in a few ways:

  • A look-alike address. The sender's address is one small change away from the real one, such as an extra letter or a hyphen.
  • A spoofed address. The message is made to look as if it came from your company or a partner, even though it didn't.
  • A hacked account. The criminal breaks into a real email account and sends the request from it. The FBI says these schemes can also use phone numbers and video meeting apps, not just email.

The FBI says criminals are now using AI tools, including voice cloning, to sound like executives. IC3 logged more than $30 million in 2025 losses to business email compromise that involved AI.

What the request usually looks like

  • Changed payment instructions. A vendor's invoice arrives with a new bank account or payment address.
  • An urgent wire. An executive needs money sent fast.
  • Gift cards. The boss needs gift cards for an event or a client and asks you to send the card numbers and PINs.
  • Real estate closings. An email that seems to come from the title company or a lawyer sends new wiring instructions for the closing.

Warning signs

  • Pressure to act quickly.
  • Any change to where money should be sent.
  • A request to pay with gift cards. The FTC says anyone who asks you to pay with a gift card is a scammer.
  • A sender address that is close to, but not exactly, the one you know.
  • Spelling mistakes, bad grammar or phrasing that doesn't sound like the sender.

Tom's advice

Make one rule and never break it: no money moves and no payment details change because of an email alone. Call the person at a phone number you already have, not one in the message, and confirm. Bosses who are real will thank you for checking. Put the rule in writing so nobody feels awkward following it.

How to protect your business

  1. Verify by phone. Confirm every payment change or unusual request by calling a number you already know. Don't reply to the email to ask.
  2. Turn on multi-factor authentication for every email account, and keep it on.
  3. Set up email authentication. The FTC recommends an email provider that supports authentication technology, which helps other servers tell real messages from your company apart from fakes.
  4. Train your staff to recognize phishing and these requests, and repeat it regularly.
  5. Share less online. The FBI warns that scammers look through social media for details that make their messages more believable.

If money has already been sent

Move fast. The FBI says time is of the essence.

  1. Call your bank right away and ask them to recall the wire or payment.
  2. File a complaint at IC3.gov, no matter how much was lost, with the full transaction details. IC3's Recovery Asset Team works with banks to freeze stolen money. Across all types of fraud in 2025, it handled 3,900 cases and froze about $679 million of roughly $1.16 billion that criminals tried to move.
  3. If gift cards were used, report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  4. Secure the email account. Warn your customers and partners. (When I clean up a hacked mailbox, I change the password right away and look for forwarding rules the criminal added to keep copies of your mail.) The FTC says a notice to customers should contain no links, so it doesn't look like phishing itself.
